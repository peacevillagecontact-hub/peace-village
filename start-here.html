Start Here

Never grown your own food, dug a well, or spent a night with no power grid? Good — this page assumes exactly that. It is a plain-language walk through everything moving off-grid and starting a regenerative life actually involves, in the order you would actually face it. Every step links to real sources if you want to go deeper, and to the forum if you want to ask the community directly.

In one paragraph: "off-grid" means your home is not connected to the public power, water, or sewer lines — you generate your own power (usually solar), get your own water (a well, spring, or rainwater), and handle your own waste. "Regenerative living" means doing this in a way that actively improves the land you are on — growing food that builds soil instead of depleting it, for example — rather than just surviving independently of it. Neither requires wilderness survival skills from day one; it requires learning a specific, learnable set of systems, one at a time, which is what the rest of this page breaks down.

Land & Shelter

1

Find Your Land

Everything starts here, and it is the hardest decision to undo. Off-grid land needs things a city apartment never makes you think about: a real water source nearby, legal road access, workable soil, and a realistic distance to help if something goes wrong. Check these on paper and in person before anything else — a beautiful view is the least important item on this list.

2

Build Your First Structures

You do not need construction experience to start. Open-source projects publish real, tested blueprints for the tools and buildings a self-sufficient village needs, built by people who have already worked out the hard parts. Basic tool skills (step 15) make this easier, but plenty of people have started with none at all.

3

Choose a Natural Building Method

Instead of trucking in lumber and concrete, many off-grid builders use the land itself — earthbag (soil-filled bags stacked and tamped into load-bearing walls), cob, or straw bale. These cost far less in materials but take more physical labor and a longer timeline — worth knowing before you commit to one.

Water & Power

4

Set Up Water

No city water line means choosing from a short list: a drilled well, a hand-dug well ("kuyu kazma"), a spring, or rainwater catchment — each with a very different cost, effort, and reliability depending on your exact land. Decide this early; it affects almost everything else, including where you can grow food or build.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
5

If You Have a Spring Above You, Use Gravity

If your land has a spring or stream higher up than your house site ("dağın tepesinde su kaynağından su çekme sistemi"), you may not need a pump or any electricity at all — water can flow downhill through pipe on gravity alone, or with a hydraulic ram pump if the drop is not quite steep enough. Once built, this needs almost no ongoing maintenance.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
6

Power It With Solar

A basic off-grid solar setup has four parts: panels (catch sunlight), a charge controller (protects the batteries), a battery bank (stores power for night and cloudy days), and an inverter (turns battery power into the electricity your devices actually use). The real skill is sizing it correctly — too small and you run out of power constantly, too large and you overspent.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
7

Heat and Cook Without a Grid

A well-built wood stove or rocket mass heater heats a home through winter on a fraction of the firewood an open fire needs, and a solar cooker can cook a full meal on a sunny day with zero fuel. Most off-grid households end up using more than one method depending on the season.

Growing & Preserving Food

8

Keep Food Without a Fridge

This is the one that trips up almost everyone from the city: you do not need electricity to keep food. A root cellar (using the naturally cool, stable temperature a few feet underground) handles root vegetables and canned goods for months — and canning, fermenting, drying, and smoking handle almost everything else.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
9

Know What to Grow, and When

Before buying a single seed, find your climate zone (the USDA system in the US, or your country's equivalent) — it tells you what can actually survive your winters and summers, and when to start seeds indoors versus sow them straight into the ground. Skipping this step is the single most common reason a first garden fails completely.

10

Grow Food Without Pesticides

Instead of spraying chemicals, permaculture leans on companion planting (plants that help each other) and attracting the insects that eat your actual pests for you. It takes more observation and patience than spraying, but keeps your soil, water, and food genuinely clean.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
11

Plant a Food Forest

A food forest copies a natural forest's layers — tall trees, smaller trees, shrubs, and ground-level plants — all planted together so they support each other and need very little ongoing work once established. It is a multi-year project, not a first-season harvest, so start it early even if you will not need it right away.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
12

Add Animals, If You Want Them

Chickens are usually the first animal people add — less space and infrastructure than anything larger, and eggs within months. Goats are a common next step for milk or land-clearing help, but need secure fencing and real attention. Neither is required for off-grid living, but both are common enough to know what you are actually signing up for before bringing one home.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
13

Understand Your Soil (And Be Skeptical of Soil Myths)

Good soil is the foundation everything above depends on — and it is also where folklore creeps in. Copper-based treatments are real and effective against plant disease, but also build up in soil over time and can harm the organisms that keep it healthy. Separately, the popular claim that copper tools carry a beneficial "energy" is not backed by evidence — worth knowing the difference before believing either claim fully.

Health, Safety & Daily Life

14

Handle Waste Without a Sewer

A composting toilet breaks down solid waste safely with no water or sewer connection and, run correctly, has no smell. Greywater — the relatively clean water from sinks and showers — can be filtered and redirected to water fruit trees instead of being wasted, which off-grid households treat as a resource, not a problem.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
15

Learn Real First Aid for When Help Is Far Away

City first aid assumes an ambulance arrives in minutes. Off-grid, help might be hours away, which changes what you actually need to know — when to improvise and keep going, and when an injury means you need to leave immediately. A real wilderness/remote first-aid course teaches exactly this difference.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
16

Stay Reachable With No Cell Signal

Many off-grid properties have no cell coverage at all. A basic radio setup — unlicensed (GMRS) for short range, or licensed amateur ("ham") radio for real distance — is how most off-grid households stay in touch with each other and the outside world.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
17

Stay Connected, If You Want To

Many off-grid properties have no cell or cable access at all. Satellite internet (Starlink being the most common) has made real internet possible almost anywhere with a clear view of the sky, and a cell signal booster can help if you have at least a weak signal to amplify. Neither is required, but most people find they want at least occasional connectivity.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
18

Learn the Everyday Skills

A surprising amount of off-grid life comes down to a short list of unglamorous physical skills: sharpening a knife or axe properly, tying the handful of knots that actually matter, lighting a fire reliably in bad weather, and basic tool maintenance. None of these require natural talent — just doing them badly a few times first.

Community, Economy & Going Further

19

Decide How Your Community Will Govern Itself

Land and systems are the easy part compared to this: how decisions get made, how work and resources are shared, and what happens when people disagree. Real communities inspired by the novel Walden Two have been running on shared labor and consensus decision-making for decades — worth studying both what worked and what did not.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
20

Consider a Local Economy

Not everything in a community has to run through money. A barter system or a time bank (trading hours of help instead of cash) lets people exchange skills and goods directly — several real communities run on exactly this, though keeping it fair over time takes real structure, not just good intentions.

See the resources on this → Join the discussion →
21

Check What Is Actually Legal Where You Are

This is the one step with no universal answer: off-grid legality depends entirely on your specific country, state or province, and county — some places have no issue at all, others have building codes that effectively require a grid connection. Research your exact jurisdiction before committing to land or a build; nothing on this page is legal advice.

22

Consider Solar-Powered Bitcoin Mining (Optional)

If your setup generates more solar power than you need, some off-grid households mine bitcoin with the surplus — but the economics only work with cheap-enough panels and batteries, strong sun hours, and realistic expectations about intermittent uptime. Treat this as a possible bonus once your essentials are solid, not a starting project.
